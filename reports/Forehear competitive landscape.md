# Forehear wins where retrieval outlasts turn-taking

As of 6 November 2026, nobody else ships what forehear ships: an open, framework-native library that runs **retrieval** on interim ASR transcripts, cancels stale guesses, checks reuse against the final transcript, counts wasted calls, and backs all of this with a deterministic benchmark on real human speech that has real streaming-ASR revisions. The pieces exist everywhere, though. A dormant Rust prototype (primd), a Rust memory engine (oneiron), a production app that hand-rolls the same loop inside Pipecat (gravitre), at least six 2025–2026 papers, and every major framework's *LLM* speculation all cover part of it. So the idea is not new, and forehear should not claim it is. The gap forehear fills is narrower and easier to defend. It is the measured decision of *when look-ahead retrieval pays*, and a reference controller that makes that decision safe to deploy. The numbers confine the value to slow paths. On a hosted vector database, retrieving once on an eager end-of-turn signal already gets the wait down to **45 ms**, against forehear's **17 ms**. Behind a reranker, forehear cuts the wait from **806 ms to 392 ms** on interim-only stacks, and beats eager-EOT retrieval by **184 ms**. On local indexes there is nothing to win. The biggest risks are structural, not code-level. Deepgram now owns Pipecat, and with it both the interim stream and the framework. LiveKit is steering RAG toward "make retrieval fast and put it in `llm_node`". Speech-to-speech APIs are moving retrieval into the model or the vendor's servers. And forehear's own LiveKit adapter currently injects context in the hook that discards LiveKit's default-on preemptive reply. The right position is "the yardstick and reference controller for look-ahead retrieval". The next moves, in order, are: fix the LiveKit hook; publish to PyPI; add a second ASR engine and named competitor baselines to the benchmark; ship a ConversationRelay adapter covering Twilio and Telnyx; then run end to end inside EVA-Bench.

## The whole loop is unclaimed, but a dozen projects ship pieces of it

forehear's own measurements define the comparison. On 4,018 HeySQuAD questions read aloud by people, streamed through Vosk in 100 ms chunks, with retrieval over 19,831 SQuAD passages, the default `stable_prefix` policy cut mean retrieval wait from **280 to 76 ms** behind a hosted knowledge base. Behind a reranker it cut wait from **806 to 392 ms**, and on a web-search path from **1,790 to 1,347 ms**. Each costs about **3.5 retrieval calls per turn** and **2.0–3.3 points of recall@5**. `ResultOverlap(0.8)` shrinks the recall cost to under 1 point, and `ResultOverlap(0.9)` is lossless on this benchmark while still saving 261 ms behind a reranker ([RESULTS](../results/RESULTS.md)). These numbers come from the production `Controller` replayed on a virtual clock. The retrieval latencies are simulated from profiles fitted to published measurements, and the speech, the ASR revisions and the retrieval results are real ([benchmark method](../docs/benchmark.md)).

Against that, the closest library-shaped rival is **rohansx/primd**. It is Rust with Python adapters, has an `observe_partial` → `finalize` API, and ships Pipecat and LiveKit adapters. But it has 3 stars, one author, and no commits since 14 May 2026. Its "100% speculative-cache hit rate" comes from random 256-bit vectors, and its own report calls that "a workload property" ([primd](https://github.com/rohansx/primd); [bench report](https://github.com/rohansx/primd/blob/main/docs/benchmarks/bench-report.md)). It has no async cancellation and one hard-coded Hamming ≤16 validator. Its LiveKit adapter subscribes to `user_speech_interim` and `user_speech_committed`, event names that current LiveKit Agents no longer emits, and it swallows the attach failure ([livekit retriever](https://github.com/rohansx/primd/blob/main/livekit-primd/livekit_primd/retriever.py); [LiveKit events.py](https://github.com/livekit/agents/blob/main/livekit-agents/livekit/agents/voice/events.py)). forehear's adapter subscribes to the current `user_input_transcribed` event. Only one competitor matches forehear's honesty about measurement: **overhear**, a single-day hackathon app. It measured a **0.318 hit rate over 22 queries** and found that "interim transcripts are revised, not merely extended". Its "spoken" queries were synthesized by TTS, and it has no licence, tests or adapters ([overhear](https://github.com/nikhilesh9ix/overhear/blob/master/README.md)).

The technically serious newcomer is **oneiron**, an Apache-2.0 Rust memory engine. Its `SpeculativeSession::observe_partial` fires prefix-only retrieval when a "meaning signature" changes, caps fires per utterance, enforces deadlines, and tags every speculative fire as wasted-retrieval budget. It has 0 stars, no Pipecat or LiveKit code, and no published hit or waste numbers ([speculative.rs](https://github.com/oneiron-dev/oneiron/blob/main/crates/oneiron/src/speculative.rs)). The most telling find is not a library at all. **gravitre-saas-backend** is an unlicensed production SaaS backend with 4,637 commits. Inside Pipecat, it hand-builds interim-triggered read-only knowledge retrieval with a 150 ms debounce and cancel-on-new-interim, alongside speculative generation with adoption rules and waste counters ([speculative_prefetch.py](https://github.com/cesarbohjr/gravitre-saas-backend/blob/main/backend/app/services/pipecat_voice/speculative_prefetch.py)). One developer spent months converging on forehear's design. That is the build-versus-buy argument in a single repository, and it shows that the real competitor for adoption is in-house code.

Everything else covers a different slice. Salesforce's **VoiceAgentRAG** (161 stars) prefetches predicted *follow-up* topics between turns. It never reads interims, and its licence is contradictory: an Apache-2.0 file sits next to a CC-BY-NC-4.0 README ([VoiceAgentRAG](https://github.com/SalesforceAIResearch/VoiceAgentRAG)). The Samsung PRISM "Streaming Live RAG" hackathon produced well-tested engines evaluated on LLM-drafted text with synthetic ASR noise ([kairos](https://github.com/cheshtarajput098-arch/kairos/blob/main/README.md)). **premove** has reserved a PyPI name with "tool/RAG prefetch (roadmap)" but ships only a docstring ([premove](https://pypi.org/project/premove/)). **pipecat-speculative-prefill** speculates KV prefill, not retrieval, from the same interim tap ([repo](https://github.com/iamdgarcia/pipecat-speculative-prefill)).

The academic field is converging on the same architecture: fixed-interval or learned triggers on partial ASR, a concurrency cap, cancellation at end of speech, and validated reuse. **Stream RAG** (Meta/CMU) promised its code and the AudioCRAG-Human benchmark 13 months ago and has released neither ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02044)). **Heartbeat Thinking** (Ant International) runs an LLM "Thinker" every 500 ms on partial ASR with at most two in flight. It cut FDBench-v3 latency from 6.90 s to 4.39 s, but lost accuracy on AudioCRAG (57.24% → 55.49%), hallucinated outdated speculative results about 4% of the time, and left cost accounting to future work ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.26233)). Qualcomm/KAIST's on-device system reports a 47.7% cache-ready rate and wasted calls cut from 47 to 5, on 146 TTS utterances ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.07641)). None of these reports retrieval calls per turn or recall@k against final-transcript retrieval. Those two quantities are forehear's core cost and quality numbers.

## Platforms speculate on the LLM and give retrieval no head start

Every mainstream framework has converged on one pattern. It speculates on *LLM generation* at a likely-end-of-turn moment, holds the output, and refuses to run tools speculatively. **LiveKit Agents** runs preemptive generation by default (`enabled: True`, LLM only, a 10 s cap, 3 retries). It fires on a final or preflight transcript, and interims can only cancel it ([turn.py](https://github.com/livekit/agents/blob/main/livekit-agents/livekit/agents/voice/turn.py); [PR #7949](https://github.com/livekit/agents/pull/7949)). Its documented RAG hook, `on_user_turn_completed`, edits the chat context. That fails the `is_equivalent` snapshot check, so the speculative reply is discarded ([agent_activity.py](https://github.com/livekit/agents/blob/main/livekit-agents/livekit/agents/voice/agent_activity.py)). The open Moss knowledge-base PR states the rule directly: retrieval "belongs in `llm_node`, not `on_user_turn_completed`: changes made there invalidate the preemptive reply" ([PR #7724](https://github.com/livekit/agents/pull/7724)). **Pipecat** speculates only on eager-EOT predictions from Deepgram Flux or Cartesia Turns. It holds the response in a `SpeculationGate`, and "Tool calls are never executed" for an unconfirmed turn. Its aggregators consume interim frames, so "a tap is the only integration point" for anything that wants partials ([CHANGELOG](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md); [issue #6511](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat/issues/6511)). NVIDIA had the only interim-triggered speculative LLM, and it is now archived; its successor blueprint has none ([ace-controller](https://github.com/NVIDIA/ace-controller); [nemotron-voice-agent](https://github.com/NVIDIA-AI-Blueprints/nemotron-voice-agent)). Dograh, a fast-growing Pipecat fork, exposes knowledge-base RAG as a tool, so its retrieval gets no head start ([dograh](https://github.com/dograh-hq/dograh)). No issue in either major framework asks for speculative *retrieval* by name. The adjacent requests, LiveKit #4881 (keep preemptive replies after context edits) and #5924 (speculation is unsafe for stateful adapters), have 0 reactions ([#4881](https://github.com/livekit/agents/issues/4881); [#5924](https://github.com/livekit/agents/issues/5924)).

Commercial platforms follow the same split. ElevenLabs' `speculativeTurn` covers "LLM responses" only. Its knowledge base runs "Every turn" after the final transcript. Its engineering team cut RAG latency from a **326 ms median to 155 ms** by racing query-rewriter models, not by starting earlier ([SDK types](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@elevenlabs/elevenlabs-js@2.39.0/api/types/TurnConfig.d.ts); [ElevenLabs engineering](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/engineering-rag)). Deepgram's Voice Agent API is the closest commercial substitute. Inside its documented "speculative window", function calls are dispatched "as soon as the LLM emits it" before the turn is confirmed, because "most functions read data, and dispatching them early is where the latency win comes from" ([Deepgram speculative replies](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/voice-agent-speculative-replies.md)). That is speculative retrieval at eager-EOT time, not during speech. Sierra describes retrieval running "in parallel and speculatively" with "predictive prefetching", but keyed on caller context such as a known customer's orders, not on partial speech ([Sierra](https://sierra.ai/blog/voice-latency)). Contact-center agent assist from Google, NICE, Genesys and Cresta markets retrieval "as the customer speaks". Google's documented triggers, though, fire per finalized message, and the human readers tolerate seconds of latency ([Google PGKA](https://docs.cloud.google.com/agent-assist/docs/pgka)). Tazo Labs blogs about firing read-only tool calls during speech and committing on an exact match, but publishes no code or measurement. Its ~480 ms figure is an illustrative calculation ([Tazo Labs](https://tazolabs.com/blog/speculative-tool-calling-voice-ai)).

Speech-to-text vendors improve forehear's inputs without competing with it. They fall into three families. Explicit stable prefixes come from Soniox final tokens and AWS `Stable` items ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/transcribe/latest/dg/streaming-partial-results.html)). Scored but unflagged interims come from Google's per-result `stability`. Whole-hypothesis rewrites come from Deepgram interims, ElevenLabs partials and Cartesia `turn.update`. **No vendor publishes revision rates**, and Artificial Analysis's streaming leaderboard measures first-partial accuracy but not stability ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/new-streaming-speech-to-text-benchmark-aa-wer-streaming)). Eager signals come from Deepgram Flux (`EagerEndOfTurn` + `TurnResumed`, off by default, at the cost of "50-70% more LLM calls"), Cartesia Ink-2 `turn.eager_end`, and LiveKit's `PREFLIGHT_TRANSCRIPT`, which plugins for Flux, AssemblyAI, Soniox and Cartesia emit ([Deepgram guide](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/flux/voice-agent-eager-eot/llms.txt); [LiveKit stt.py](https://github.com/livekit/agents/blob/055744e1505b1489976d06ca029b3001bc91ea55/livekit-agents/livekit/agents/stt/stt.py)). The market has consolidated sharply. **Deepgram announced its acquisition of Daily, the maker of Pipecat, on 29 October 2026.** It pledged that Pipecat stays BSD-licensed and vendor-neutral under a foundation that has no charter yet ([Deepgram blog](https://deepgram.com/blog/daily-pipecat-deepgram); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/29/deepgram-acquires-pipecat-maker-daily-to-bolster-its-voice-ai-infrastructure/)). The Real-Time Context Standard (RCS) it launched with Pipecat is irrelevant to forehear: it is paralinguistic markup, and "a producer MUST write only the final reading of a turn" ([RCS spec](https://docs.pipecat.ai/api-reference/standards/real-time-context-standard)).

Speech-to-speech is the slow-moving architectural threat. Every commercial S2S API grounds knowledge through tool calls the model decides to make. That leaves the app one lever: answer the tool call from a cache it already filled. On Full-Duplex-Bench-v3, GPT-Realtime and Gemini Live take **2.2–4.6 s from the end of a disfluent utterance to the first tool call** (Grok is an outlier at 0.63 s), which leaves ample room for prefetch ([FDB-v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04847v1)). The seam differs by vendor. OpenAI Realtime's in-session transcription starts on commit, and developers report no deltas "until the user stops talking". Its streaming `gpt-live-transcribe` model is "Not supported" on `v1/realtime` and needs a parallel session at $0.017/min ([OpenAI transcription](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-transcription.md); [forum](https://community.openai.com/t/realtime-streaming-transcription/1371205); [model page](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-live-transcribe.md)). Gemini Live documents `interimInputTranscription` "updated while the user is speaking", but only clearly for its transcribe model ([Gemini Live reference](https://ai.google.dev/api/live)). Where the vendor runs the retrieval itself, forehear's ceiling is zero. Remote MCP tools "are executed by the Realtime API itself", and Gemini's Google Search grounding is server-side ([OpenAI Realtime MCP](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-mcp)). Research models go further: MoshiRAG emits its own retrieval token and keeps talking while retrieval runs ([moshi-rag](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/moshi-rag)). The countervailing evidence is soft but consistent. Cascades remain the production default, according to Deepgram and Gradium, which both have commercial stakes ([Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/blog/speech-to-speech-vs-cascade-voice-agent-architecture); [Gradium](https://gradium.ai/content/cascaded-voice-agent-vs-speech-to-speech-2026)). NVIDIA's frontend-backend design streams ASR text to a text backend that makes the tool calls, which is exactly forehear's input ([arXiv 2609.19334](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19334)).

The pattern in the table is the strategic point. Every party that owns a seam speculates on the LLM, which is the expensive component. Nobody speculates on retrieval, which is the cheap one. And every first-party RAG answer moves retrieval into the speculated branch rather than ahead of it.

## Faster retrieval erases forehear's win in three cases and leaves it everywhere else

forehear's value scales with retrieval latency, so the honest substitute map is a latency map. In three situations forehear is fully substituted. The first is a fully in-process path with no reranker. forehear's own `local` profile drops from 10 ms to 2 ms, and its `Adaptive` policy switches itself off at 1.01 calls per turn ([RESULTS](../results/RESULTS.md)). The second is a knowledge base small enough to live in a cached prompt. With a 10,000-token system prompt, caching improved time-to-first-token only 6–31%, and at 50K tokens GPT-4o still took **1.7 s**. That puts the practical "inline it" ceiling at roughly 5–10K tokens, an inferred threshold ([arXiv 2601.06007](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06007v2)). The third is a response-cache hit, which skips both retrieval and the LLM. No vendor publishes voice hit rates, and the most conservative text-support figures are 10–15% ([arXiv 2510.26835](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26835v1)). Cloudflare's gateway cache is exact-match only, so it almost never hits ASR text ([Cloudflare docs mirror](https://03cfcb76.previews.developers.cloudflare.com/ai-gateway/features/caching/)). LiveKit's Moss plugin is the first-party version of the in-process substitute: "a search takes about a millisecond and makes no network request" ([PR #7724](https://github.com/livekit/agents/pull/7724)).

Outside those cases, 2026's measured latencies support forehear's profiles and leave real time to hide. A Voyage embedding call alone has a median of **134–150 ms**. Voyage rerank-2.5 has a median of **~330 ms at K=50 and ~480 ms at K=100**, with provider-side spikes to 2.9 s ([arXiv 2511.09545](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.09545v1)). ElevenLabs' own voice RAG ran 326 ms median and 629 ms p95 before optimization ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/engineering-rag)). These all sit inside forehear's `hosted_kb` (250/550 ms) and `rerank` (750/1,400 ms) profiles. Cold starts make the tails longer. In a Zilliz-run benchmark, Pinecone Serverless's first query after idle took 271 ms against 60 ms warm, and unpinned Turbopuffer took **2,048 ms** against 322 ms ([Zilliz VDBBench](https://zilliz.com/blog/vdbbench-adds-cost-aware-benchmarking-for-vector-databases.md)). A first-turn query fired during the caller's speech absorbs that penalty, which is a selling point forehear's benchmark does not yet model. One profile is pessimistic. `web_search` at a 1,600 ms median is 2–4× slower than 2026's fast tiers (Exa Instant ~398 ms, Parallel Turbo ~348 ms mean), though it fits Tavily basic (1.88 s) or search plus page fetch ([Parallel.ai](https://parallel.ai/articles/best-web-search-api-for-ai-applications.md)).

The substitute that matters most is one forehear already contains: retrieving once on an eager end-of-turn signal. forehear's results show it captures most of the hosted-KB win. Wait falls from 280 ms to **45 ms at 1.13 calls per turn**. forehear's best policy reaches 17 ms, but only by spending roughly 2.8 more calls per turn to save 28 ms ([RESULTS](../results/RESULTS.md)). The gap widens on slow paths: 507 ms against 323 ms behind a reranker (184 ms better), and 1,490 ms against 1,327 ms on web search (163 ms better; 227 ms with `stable_prefix_overlap`). Two more facts shape the picture. First, faster turn detection shrinks forehear's head start. Moving end of turn from 500 ms to 300 ms after the last word cuts hosted-KB savings from 203 ms to 173 ms, and LiveKit's detector already needs only 295 ms at a 10% false-cutoff rate ([RESULTS](../results/RESULTS.md); [LiveKit](https://livekit.com/blog/solving-end-of-turn-detection)). Second, forehear's strongest cases, rerankers and web search, are exactly where each extra call costs real money. At ~3.5 calls per turn and a speculative accuracy of 24%, reranker or search-API spend rises roughly 3.5×. The benchmark reports this as calls per turn but never translates it into dollars, and a buyer will.

Most of the rest of the latency toolbox stacks with forehear rather than replacing it. Proximity's approximate query cache cut database calls by 77.2% on skewed workloads ([arXiv 2503.05530](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05530v3)). forehear's successive interim queries are near-duplicates, which is exactly the workload that cache handles best, so pairing the two attacks forehear's main cost objection. VoiceAgentRAG covers the window *between* turns. It always misses turn 1 and hits only 58% on turns 1–4 ([arXiv 2603.02206](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02206v2)), which is precisely forehear's territory, so the two could share one cache. KV and prefix reuse (CacheBlend, vLLM APC, SGLang) cut LLM prefill. Once forehear's top-k stabilizes before end of turn, a self-hosted stack could prefill the retrieved chunks while the user is still talking. No system found does this, which makes it an unclaimed niche, along with speculative semantic-cache lookup on interims.

## Benchmarks score whole agents, never retrieval readiness or waste

No public benchmark scores whether retrieved context is ready at end of turn, how many speculative calls were spent, or how real ASR revisions break reuse. **EVA-Bench** (ServiceNow, MIT, 236 stars) measures the end-to-end latency forehear aims to reduce. Its paper lists "speculative execution, response pre-fetching, streaming-aware scheduling" as unused. Its code logs Cartesia's eager-to-final delta as "a possible future enhancement". Its leaderboard runs ElevenLabs and Cartesia with vendor speculation on, without isolating the effect ([EVA paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.13841); [EVA repo](https://github.com/ServiceNow/eva); [experiment setup](https://github.com/ServiceNow/eva/blob/main/docs/experiment_setup.md)). **Full-Duplex-Bench-v3** is the only benchmark that counts tool calls "invoked before the user finishes speaking", from 10.8% for GPT-Realtime to 41.6% for Grok. It does not grade whether those calls were right or wasted, and it has 100 recordings from 12 speakers ([FDB-v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04847)). **Galbraith's** stabilization analysis is the nearest methodology. It finds 73.9% of CRAG queries "streamable" at a 600 ms tool latency, but on uniform simulated word streams, and it names "real ASR pacing is variable, and partial hypotheses get revised" as an open limitation ([arXiv 2606.20113](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.20113)). forehear's lower **43% retrievable one second before the last word** on real Vosk interims is the speech-real answer to that limitation. The two use different metrics and corpora, though, so they are not directly comparable.

| Benchmark | Real human speech | Real interim revisions | Retrieval | Ready-at-EOT and waste metrics | Runs without API keys |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **forehear bench** ([method](../docs/benchmark.md)) | 4,018 read questions | Yes (Vosk) | BM25 or dense, gold passages | Yes: wait, ready-at-EOT, coverage, accuracy, calls/turn, recall Δ | Yes, CPU, deterministic |
| [EVA-Bench](https://github.com/ServiceNow/eva) | No (simulated callers) | No | No (mock tool DB) | No; latency diagnostic only | No |
| [FDB-v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04847) | 100 recordings | n/a (S2S) | Mock APIs | Pre-emptive call rate, ungraded | No (LLM judges) |
| [τ-Voice / τ-Knowledge](https://github.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench) | Simulated voice | No | ~700-document KB | No latency metric | No |
| [Galbraith](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.20113) | No | No (uniform text stream) | BM25 and dense, CRAG | Stabilization bound; no call counts | Yes |
| [Stream RAG AudioCRAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02044) | 618 (unreleased) | No (forced-aligned truncation) | Web and KG | Latency and LLM-judged accuracy | No |

The benchmark's own blind spots are just as concrete, and competitors will name them. It uses single-turn, read-aloud SQuAD questions, which were written while looking at the passage, so their prefixes overlap lexically with the gold passage. That likely flatters early retrievability. It uses one ASR engine, and Vosk revises differently from Deepgram or Whisper-streaming. Its latencies are modelled, and its recall is measured with BM25 even under the rerank profile ([benchmark limitations](../docs/benchmark.md)). One roadmap item needs correcting. The README names DSTC10 Track 2 as the multi-turn extension, but a clone of that dataset contains **ASR text and n-best lists with no audio and no word timings**. So it cannot drive a real-interim replay without new recordings ([DSTC10 repo](https://github.com/alexa/alexa-with-dstc10-track2-dataset)). SpokenWOZ has audio but is CC BY-NC and has no passage labels. No permissively licensed multi-turn spoken dataset with passage labels exists. Recording one would itself be a contribution.

Distribution data warns that a benchmark alone does not spread. LiveKit's eot-bench has 73 stars and VoiceAgentRAG 158, while `livekit-plugins-turn-detector` draws about **1.18M downloads a month** because framework templates include it by default. vocode has 3.8k stars and just 890 monthly downloads ([market data via pypistats](https://pypistats.org/api/packages/livekit-plugins-turn-detector/recent); [ecosyste.ms](https://repos.ecosyste.ms/api/v1/hosts/GitHub/repositories/pipecat-ai/pipecat)). The single HN voice-latency post that broke out (570 points) was a per-stage teardown with measured numbers, and its comments framed latency as "an orchestration problem" ([HN 47224295](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47224295)).

Two cross-cutting risks sit outside the table. First, privacy. Speculative calls send unfinished, possibly mis-recognized user intent to external services, a point Tazo raises explicitly ([Tazo Labs](https://tazolabs.com/blog/speculative-tool-calling-voice-ai)). forehear's read-only rule addresses side effects, not data leakage. Second, demand is conditional. Retrieval latency is a top pain mainly for knowledge- and tool-heavy agents. One LiveKit user measured 3.3 s of silence on tool turns against 2.6 s on normal turns ([LiveKit #3824](https://github.com/livekit/agents/issues/3824)), but the general builder conversation centers on endpointing and LLM time-to-first-token.

## Claim the yardstick, not the idea

The defensible positioning is: **"forehear measures when look-ahead retrieval pays and what it costs, under real streaming-ASR revisions, and ships the read-only controller that passed the test."** Everything unique to forehear sits in that sentence. It has interchangeable trigger policies, including the eager-EOT and Stream-RAG-style timer baselines, in one harness. It validates on retrieved results, not just text. It reports calls per turn and speculative accuracy. And it runs on thousands of real recordings rather than TTS or simulated text. A framework vendor could copy the controller in a release cycle, but it would also need to copy the evidence. forehear's runtime also contains its strongest substitute as a configuration: `EagerEndOfTurn` is a built-in policy, and `Adaptive` turns itself off when retrieval is fast. So forehear can pitch itself as the place retrieval timing is decided, rather than as one timing choice competing with others.

| Claim | Make it? | Defensible wording, or why not |

It should cite VoiceAgentRAG, Stream RAG and SHANKS as related work and answer the three predictable objections up front: unstable partials, data leaving on speculative branches, and per-call cost.

## Conclusion

The market has an inverted shape, and that shape is forehear's opening. Engineering effort has gone into speculating on the most expensive component, the LLM, at the latest moment, eager end of turn. Retrieval is the cheapest thing to speculate on, and it stays serial everywhere except in a handful of prototypes and one hand-built SaaS backend. The reason is not ignorance of the idea, which hackathons now set as homework. Nobody has shown practitioners where it pays. forehear's own data gives the answer most vendors would rather not hear: for a hosted KB on a stack with an eager signal, speculation buys about 28 ms. Saying so plainly is the moat, because it makes the slow-path gains believable: 414–443 ms on interim-only stacks behind a reranker or web search, and 163–184 ms beyond eager-EOT retrieval.

The strategic risk is less that someone builds a better forehear than that the seam closes. LiveKit could make retrieval fast enough not to need speculation. Deepgram could fold it into Pipecat. And S2S vendors could run retrieval where apps cannot see it. All three point to the same response: be the neutral, evidence-backed reference those platforms adopt, rather than a library they replace. Concretely, that means compatibility with LiveKit's default preemptive path within days, named rival baselines and a second ASR engine within weeks, and an EVA-Bench result within a quarter. The open question that will decide forehear's ceiling is whether the 43% early-retrievability figure holds on spontaneous, multi-turn speech from rewrite-style recognizers. No public dataset can answer that yet, and forehear is the project best placed to build one.
